# Hackathon Format Playbook

Use this format for hackathons, build nights, developer challenges, AI build days, student hacker events, internal innovation sprints, partner API challenges, and technical community competitions. Load `../channels/events-sales.md`, `event-hosting.md`, and `event-promotion-follow-up.md` first.

## Source Guidance

- Major League Hacking organizer guide: https://guide.mlh.io/
- MLH venue guidance: https://guide.mlh.io/general-information/locking-down-a-venue
- MLH event logistics guidance: https://guide.mlh.io/general-information/event-logistics/set-up-your-event
- FTC endorsement guidance for sponsors and prizes: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides

## Use Cases

- Build developer goodwill, seed an ecosystem, drive API/product adoption, recruit builders, create demo artifacts, or gather product feedback under high-energy conditions.
- Run small focused build events as well as larger weekend hackathons.
- Prepare sponsor, mentor, judge, participant, and operator materials that keep the event fair and survivable.

## Required Inputs

- Hackathon thesis: theme, builder audience, skill level, product/API constraints, eligible project types, expected team size, and what a good project looks like.
- Format: evening build night, one-day hackathon, weekend hackathon, internal sprint, online challenge, hybrid challenge, or demo day.
- Operations: venue capacity, WiFi load, power, tables, presentation space, food, beverages, check-in, quiet area, hardware area, sponsor area, mentor area, judging area, first-aid/safety owner, and overnight policy if any.
- Rules: eligibility, team formation, IP ownership, code of conduct, project submission requirements, judging criteria, prize terms, sponsor API requirements, data/privacy constraints, and anti-cheating policy.
- People plan: organizers, MC, mentors, technical support, sponsor reps, judges, photographers, food runners, security/contact person, and escalation owner.
- Schedule: kickoff, team formation, workshops, hacking blocks, meals, mentor hours, submission deadline, demo order, judging, awards, and teardown.

## Output Shape

- Hackathon brief: theme, audience, promise, rules, prizes, sponsor/partner notes, success criteria, and risk flags.
- Participant page copy: who it is for, what to bring, what can be built, schedule, rules, judging criteria, food/accessibility notes, and CTA.
- Operator runbook: staffing, room map, supplies, signage, AV/WiFi/power, meals, sponsor areas, mentor shifts, judging process, and incident plan.
- Workshop/mentor plan: technical sessions, office hours, API examples, starter repo/demo, troubleshooting path, and escalation contacts.
- Judging pack: rubric, score sheet, judge briefing, demo timing, conflict-of-interest rules, prize announcement copy, and winner follow-up.
- Post-event pack: participant thank-you, project showcase, winner recap, sponsor proof, recruiting/product follow-up, and retro.

## Copy And Creative Rules

- Make the build constraint exciting. A hackathon without a clear creative boundary becomes unfocused and hard to judge.
- Explain what level of builder can succeed. Beginners need starter paths; advanced builders need enough challenge to care.
- Put rules in plain language. Participants should understand ownership, prizes, eligibility, judging, and sponsor/API requirements before they register.
- Promote the artifact, not just the event. "Leave with a working agent workflow prototype" is stronger than "learn about agents."
- Give sponsors useful roles without letting them hijack the event. Sponsor workshops, office hours, prizes, or API challenges should serve builders.
- Write schedules with buffers. Meals, WiFi issues, project setup, judging delays, and demo transitions take longer than planned.

## Psychological Levers

- Builders respond to autonomy plus constraint. Give a theme, judging frame, and starter materials without over-prescribing the solution.
- Fairness is motivational. Clear rules, transparent judging, and conflict-of-interest handling protect trust.
- Momentum depends on early wins. Starter repos, API keys, sample prompts, templates, and mentor availability reduce abandonment.
- Public demo deadlines create focus, but the submission process must be simple enough not to punish good projects.
- Recognition matters beyond prizes. Showcase posts, demo videos, sponsor intros, and recruiting follow-up extend the value.

## Platform Adaptation

- Student hackathon: stronger code of conduct, travel/overnight/safety planning, beginner support, team formation, and sponsor mentoring.
- Developer community build night: shorter schedule, fewer prizes, stronger API examples, more peer learning, and lightweight demos.
- Corporate innovation sprint: business problem statements, data/privacy rules, internal stakeholder judging, and implementation follow-up.
- Online hackathon: more asynchronous support, clear submission rubric, office hours, community moderation, and timezone planning.
- Partner API challenge: sponsor enablement, sandbox credentials, example apps, technical support SLA, and disclosure of sponsor influence.

## Variants And Testing

- Test theme specificity, prize structure, workshop topics, team formation method, mentor density, judging rubric, and submission friction.
- Track registrations, check-ins, team formation, submissions, demos completed, API/product usage, mentor requests, project quality, participant satisfaction, sponsor satisfaction, recruiting leads, and post-event product activation.
- Review where teams got stuck. That usually reveals missing docs, weak examples, confusing APIs, or insufficient mentor coverage.
- Keep a project gallery and source-of-truth outcome log; hackathon value often compounds through demos and relationships after the event.

## Review Checklist

- Theme, audience, eligibility, rules, and judging criteria are clear.
- Venue, WiFi, power, food, beverages, safety, signage, and staffing are operationally realistic.
- Code of conduct, IP ownership, prize terms, sponsor requirements, and data/privacy constraints are explicit.
- Starter materials, API access, mentor plan, and troubleshooting path are ready.
- Submission and demo process is simple and timed.
- Sponsor value and participant value are balanced.
- Post-event showcase, sponsor proof, and follow-up owners are defined.

## Failure Diagnostics

- Few submissions: theme too broad, setup too hard, API/docs weak, mentor coverage low, or schedule unrealistic.
- Bad demos: judging criteria unclear, submission process confusing, demo timing too short, or teams lacked presentation guidance.
- Participant frustration: WiFi/power failure, food gaps, unclear rules, sponsor pressure, or no support path.
- Sponsor disappointment: deliverables vague, poor signage, weak workshop attendance, no proof pack, or no follow-up.
- No lasting value: projects not captured, winners not showcased, product team not briefed, or recruiting/community follow-up missing.

## Anti-Patterns

- Calling any workshop with a prize a hackathon.
- Overloading participants with sponsor pitches before they can build.
- Hiding rules until judging.
- Underestimating WiFi, power, food, and staffing.
- Judging on vibes instead of a rubric.
- Letting good projects disappear after demos.
